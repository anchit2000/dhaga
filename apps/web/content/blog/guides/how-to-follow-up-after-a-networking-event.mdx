You leave a good event on a high. Startup Mahakumbh, a TiE dinner, a Friday demo day in Bengaluru, a product meetup where three conversations actually went somewhere. Your jacket pocket is full of cards, your LinkedIn has thirty new requests, and you tell yourself you'll follow up with all of them this weekend.

Then the weekend comes. You open your phone and it's a wall of first names with no context. Rahul from — where? The woman who runs a fund and said something smart about pricing — what was her name? The founder who offered to intro you to a customer — gone. You send four safe, generic "great to connect!" messages, feel vaguely guilty about the rest, and move on. Three weeks later those thirty warm connections are strangers again.

This guide is about fixing that. Not with a cleverer template — though you'll get templates — but by fixing the actual reason post-event follow-up breaks.

## Why follow-ups actually fail (and it isn't the wording)

Search "how to follow up after a networking event" and you'll get a hundred articles about subject lines and tone. Useful, but they're solving the wrong problem. The wording is the last 5% of the job.

The real failure is upstream. By the time you sit down to write, the context is already gone. You met forty people across six hours. You had genuine, specific conversations — and every one of them has quietly evaporated. What's left is a name and maybe a company. And a follow-up built on "name + company" can only ever be generic, because that's all you've got.

> A contact with no context is functionally a stranger. The follow-up feels forced because, honestly, it is — you're writing to someone you no longer remember.

This is a memory-and-organisation problem wearing a copywriting costume. The people who are great at post-event follow-up aren't better writers. They just don't lose the context. They walk out of the room with the conversation captured somewhere reliable — who this person is, what you actually talked about, and the one thing worth following up on — so that a week later the message writes itself.

Everything below assumes you've solved that. If you haven't, skip to [the system that makes this automatic](#the-part-nobody-tells-you-its-the-system-not-the-message) first, then come back for the templates.

## How long should you wait to follow up?

The single most common question, so here's a straight answer: **send the first, personalised message within 24 to 48 hours.** While the event is still fresh — for both of you. Wait a week and you're a stranger reintroducing yourself; wait a month and you're starting over.

But "how long to wait to follow up" isn't one decision, it's a cadence. One message and silence is how connections go cold. A relentless drip is how you get muted. The sweet spot is a small number of well-spaced, low-pressure touches, each one carrying something the other person might actually want.

The first follow-up is the one that matters most, which is why it's highlighted above. Nail the 24–48 hour note and you've earned the right to everything after it. Miss it, and the rest of the cadence never starts.

## How to write the follow-up email

The whole game is specificity. A generic message ("Great to meet you, let's stay in touch!") is indistinguishable from spam and gets the same treatment. A message that references one concrete thing from your conversation proves you were actually present — and that's the entire point.

Keep it short, lead with the callback, and don't bury an ask in the first message. Here are templates you can copy and adapt. Replace the bracketed bits with real details — that's the work.

**1. The 24–48 hour personalised note (email or LinkedIn DM)**

> Hi [Name] — really enjoyed our chat about [specific topic] at [event]. [One specific callback, e.g. "Your point about onboarding for Tier-2 SaaS buyers has been rattling around my head since."] Would genuinely like to stay in touch — no agenda. [Optional: "Here's the [article / tool / person] I mentioned: [link]."]

**2. The Day 3–5 value share**

> Hi [Name] — came across [thing] and immediately thought of what you said about [problem]. [One line on why it's relevant.] No need to reply, just thought it might be useful.

**3. The Day 14 light check-in**

> Hey [Name] — how did [thing they mentioned they were working on] end up going? Been curious since we spoke.

**4. The Day 30+ reconnect**

> Hi [Name] — been a bit. [One relevant update or a genuine question about their world.] Would love to properly catch up if you're up for a call sometime.

Subject lines do a surprising amount of work — they decide whether the note gets opened at all. Keep them human and specific:

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| "Following up" | "Great meeting you at [event]" |
| "Nice to connect" | "That [topic] you mentioned — found something" |
| "Touching base" | "The [tool/intro] I promised" |
| "Checking in" | "How did [their project] go?" |

The pattern across all four templates: reference something specific, give more than you ask, and make it effortless to reply — or to not reply. Which brings us to the anxiety underneath most of this.

## How to follow up without being pushy

The fear of being pushy is why so many good follow-ups never get sent. The reframe: pushy isn't about frequency, it's about direction. A message that gives something — a relevant article, an introduction, a useful answer — is welcome even weekly. A message that only extracts — "just circling back," "any update?", "did you get a chance to look?" — feels pushy the second time and desperate the third.

A few rules that keep you on the right side of the line:

- **Lead with value, not the ask.** Your first two or three touches should give something and request nothing. Earn the right to ask before you ask.
- **Always leave an easy exit.** "No need to reply" is not weakness — it signals you respect their time and aren't keeping score.
- **Be specific, every time.** Specificity reads as respect. It proves you remember them as a person, not a lead in a pipeline.
- **Space it out.** The cadence above is deliberate. If you feel the urge to send a second message the next day, that urge is the pushiness — sit on it.
- **Let the non-repliers go — for now.** No reply isn't rejection; it's usually a full inbox. Note it, move on, and a genuine reason to reach out will surface in a month.

Do this and "follow up without being pushy" stops being a tightrope. You're not chasing anyone — you're just being a useful person to know, at a sustainable pace.
