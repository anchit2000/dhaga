Every Indian founder eventually hits the same wall. The runway math stops being comfortable, you decide to raise, and your first honest thought is: *I don't actually know any VCs.* So you draft a cold email to a partner at a fund you admire, hit send, and it lands in a folder with four hundred other cold emails that will never be read.

Here's the thing you already suspect: you were never as far from that investor as it felt. Somewhere in your phone, your WhatsApp, your old colleagues, and your last three events is a person who knows them — and would happily say a good word. You just can't see the path. This guide is about making that path visible, and then walking it well.

## Why warm intros beat cold outreach

Nobody needs convincing that a warm intro works better than a cold DM. Investors say it constantly, and it's commonly cited that most seed rounds start with a warm connection rather than a form submission. Treat the specific numbers you'll see floating around — "4x more likely," "X% of rounds start warm" — as folklore, not data. The mechanism, though, is real and easy to reason about.

A cold email asks a busy stranger to spend trust they don't have on you yet. A warm introduction *borrows* trust that already exists between two people. When a founder the investor already backed says "you should meet this person," the investor isn't evaluating you from zero — they're extending an existing relationship. That's the whole trick. You're not skipping the evaluation; you're starting it from a warmer place.

The matrix is deliberately fair: cold outreach scales in a way warm intros never will. You can send a hundred cold emails in an afternoon and you cannot ask for a hundred warm intros without burning every relationship you have. The point isn't "never go cold." It's that for the handful of people who genuinely matter — a lead investor, a first enterprise customer, a key hire — a warm path is worth the effort to find.

## Find mutual connections: the shortest-path idea

Networks are graphs. You sit at the center, the people you know form your first degree, the people *they* know form your second degree, and the person you want to reach is a node somewhere out there. "Finding mutual connections" is really a graph question: **what is the shortest path from you to the target, and who's on it?**

The shortest path matters because every hop costs trust. A two-hop path — you know Priya, Priya knows the investor — is a single, comfortable ask. A four-hop path, where the intro gets relayed through people who barely remember each other, dilutes to nothing by the time it arrives. Fewer intermediaries, stronger vouch.

Read the map above. There are two real routes to Meera, the investor you want. The long one runs you → Arjun → Kabir → Meera: three hops, three favors, three chances for the thread to go cold. The amber route runs you → Priya → Meera: two hops, one ask. Priya is a *mutual connection* — she's in your first degree and she knows Meera directly. Everyone else (Neha, Farida, Dev, Sana) is a genuine part of your network with no useful path to this particular target. That's normal. A network is mostly dead ends for any given goal; the skill is finding the one short path that isn't.

The catch: **most of us cannot see this map.** LinkedIn will show you a "2nd" badge next to Meera, but it won't tell you that Priya is the right asker and Kabir the wrong one — because it doesn't know that Priya and Meera actually *like* each other, or that Kabir owes Meera a favor he'd rather not spend on you. The signal that decides whether an intro works lives in your private context, not in a public directory.

## Map your own network first

Before you can find the shortest path, you need the map. For most founders the raw material is already there — it's just scattered:

- **Your phone and WhatsApp** — the ex-colleague who moved to a fund, the acquaintance who "does a bit of angel stuff."
- **Old event contacts** — the person from that demo day who offered to help and then fell out of your memory.
- **Your LinkedIn connections** — useful as a roster, even if the graph it draws is public and context-free.

The work is turning that pile into something you can query. A few honest ways to do it:

1. **Write down context, not just names.** "Priya — founding team at [fund], warm, met twice, offered to intro" is worth more than a phone number. The context is what tells you she's the right asker.
2. **Note who knows whom.** Every time someone mentions a connection — "oh, I used to work with Meera" — that's an edge in your graph. Capture it.
3. **Ask the map, not your memory.** The reason the wall feels so tall at 1 a.m. is that you're doing graph traversal in your head, and human memory is terrible at it. A written, searchable graph does the traversal for you.

> Your network is almost never too small. It just isn't written down — and a connection with no context is functionally a stranger.

## The double opt-in intro: how to actually ask

Once you've found your Priya, don't ask her to blast the investor blind. Use the **double opt-in** — the standard, respectful way to run a warm intro, and the one most investors quietly prefer.

**Step one:** ask the connector for permission to be introduced, and hand them a forwardable blurb. The blurb is the whole game. Priya is busy; if you make her write the pitch, the intro dies in her drafts. Write it *for* her, in the third person, so she can forward it with one line:

> *Sharing [Founder], who's building [one-sentence what]. They just [concrete proof point — a real number, a named customer, a milestone]. They're raising a [stage] round and I thought of you because [specific reason this investor, not a generic flattery]. Happy to intro if you're open to it — no pressure.*

Keep it to four or five sentences. Make the "why you" specific — thesis fit, a portfolio company, a stage they lead. Generic blurbs read as spray-and-pray and the connector can feel it.

**Step two:** Priya forwards your blurb to Meera and asks, "Want an intro?" This is the second opt-in. If Meera says yes, Priya connects you. If Meera's quiet, nothing happened — no one was put on the spot, and Priya's relationship with Meera is intact. That protection is exactly *why* connectors are willing to make intros at all. Respect it and you'll get asked again; violate it and you won't.

A few rules that keep you welcome:

- **Never CC the target without their opt-in.** Forcing a "surprise" intro burns the connector, not just you.
- **Make the connector look good.** They're spending their credibility. Show up prepared, reply fast, and close the loop so they know it went well.
- **One clear ask.** "Would you introduce me to Meera for our seed round?" beats "do you know anyone who invests?"

## How to give intros well

The fastest way to earn warm intros is to give them. Connectors remember who made them look good and who wasted their time, and the founders with the deepest networks are almost always generous introducers themselves.

Give intros the way you'd want to receive them: **always double opt-in.** Ask both sides before you connect them. Send a two-line "why you two should talk" that makes the value obvious. And be honest about strength — "I've worked with her, she's excellent" is a real vouch; "we're LinkedIn connections" is not, and pretending otherwise spends trust you'll want back later.

The compounding effect is the point. Every good intro you make is a small deposit into a network that will, when you need it, produce a Priya of your own. ## Takeaway

Warm introductions win because they borrow trust that already exists. The bottleneck is never the intro itself — it's finding the shortest path to the person you want to reach, and most of us can't see that path because it lives in scattered, un-written context. Write the map down, find your two-hop connector, ask with a clean double opt-in, and give as many good intros as you get. Do that consistently and the wall at 1 a.m. — *I don't know any VCs* — stops being true.
