**Dhaga** turns everyone you meet into a private knowledge graph you own. You
capture a person once — from a card photo, a pasted email signature, a voice
note — and Dhaga keeps the source as a receipt, extracts the facts, and wires
them into a graph you can search, ask questions of, and explore visually.

This guide walks through the app the way you actually use it. Each page is a
short "how to" for one part of the product.

## Your daily briefing

`/app` opens on the Home briefing — the short list of **threads to pull
today**, alongside the follow-ups you set and the people you touched most
recently. Dhaga weighs everyone in your graph against everything it knows is
actually happening — a keep-in-touch rhythm that has come due, a follow-up
dated for today, a birthday landing, a job change you asked to watch for, the
goal you're working on, how long the silence has run, how connected someone is
— and shows you the few at the top. Every row says why it's there:

- **Check-in** — someone you keep in touch with daily.
- **Due** — your keep-in-touch rhythm for this person has run out.
- **Follow-up** — something you promised to do is due today, or is late.
- **Occasion** — a birthday or anniversary, today or just ahead.
- **Goal** — they're part of the shortlist for the goal you set, and you
  haven't reached them yet.
- **Signal** — a job change or news story from your watchlist.
- **Quiet** — no reason beyond the silence; you simply haven't spoken in a
  long while.
- **Network** — nothing is due at all; they're well connected in your graph
  and worth a thread anyway.

Being **starred** nudges someone up the list, and the list rotates gently day
to day so the same well-connected face isn't there every morning — but neither
ever becomes the stated reason. "Last spoke" means more than the button:
writing a note about someone, or scanning them at an event, resets their
rhythm exactly as **Reached out** does. Each row has a one-tap **Reached out**
button so the list stays honest, and on a day already full of meetings the
list gets shorter — never empty.

Once your graph isn't empty, a **graph at a glance** strip
renders beneath the briefing — quick counts of People, Companies, Notes, Facts,
Relationships, Events, Open follow-ups, and Entities. When AI extraction needs a
decision from you, a **Confirmations** preview tile surfaces the count of pending
confirmations waiting on your yes/no. The capture dock sits at
the bottom of every screen —
**Voice**, **Camera**, **Upload**, and **Capture** — so you can add someone the
moment you meet them.

## Working toward a goal

**Set a goal** sits at the top of the Today tile. Say what you're actually
trying to do in one plain sentence — *"reach out to VCs"*, *"reconnect with
people from the Delhi trip"* — and Dhaga turns it into a finite shortlist of
people **from your own graph** rather than leaving it as an intention. Overnight
it searches your notes, titles and companies for who might fit, weighs each
candidate against the sentence you wrote, and from then on feeds a few of them
into the briefing each morning as **Goal** rows. The reason line hands your
objective back to you with how many people are left.

The goal itself then sits above the list as a strip: your sentence, a bar
showing *N of M done*, and a menu to **Edit**, **Mark done**, or **Archive** it.
There is no separate goals page — a goal has one list to feed and one number
worth reading, and both are here.

**One goal at a time.** Setting a new one closes the old one, because a goal is
what Home is currently about and two of them would turn the briefing into a list
of lists. **The shortlist is finite**, so it ends: you work through it a few
people a day and then it is done, rather than a feed that refills forever.

**It burns down on any real contact, not just the button.** Someone drops off
the shortlist the moment your last contact with them moves past the day they
were shortlisted — so writing a note about them, or scanning them at an event,
closes them out exactly as **Reached out** does. The bar and the list can never
disagree, because both are counted the same way.

Phrase the goal the way your notes are phrased. Dhaga finds candidates by
matching the words in your objective against what's actually written in your
graph, so *"designers in Bangalore"* or *"people to see on my Delhi trip"*
recall well, while something abstract like *"people who could open doors"*
recalls poorly — nothing in your graph is worded that way.

## People Dhaga doesn't suggest

An imported address book is never only people: it arrives carrying "Ola
Support", "Vegetable Vendor", "AC Repair". Dhaga reads over the contacts you've
never done anything with and marks the ones that look like a business, a shop
or a support line rather than someone you could message, and stops offering
them on the surfaces where it volunteers a name — the daily briefing, the
going-quiet list, recommendations, the graph filler.

**Nothing is hidden or deleted.** A contact marked that way is still in
**People**, still in search, still in duplicate detection and merging, still in
your Wrapped card, and still in every export. The only thing that changes is
that Dhaga stops putting it in front of you unasked. A row you went looking for
is always there.

**You can see the whole list, and overrule it.** The People page carries an *"N
hidden from suggestions"* link that opens exactly those rows; each contact's own
page shows whether the call was **Dhaga's guess** or **you marked this**, and
one tap flips it. The People table's bulk bar can mark many at once. Your
ruling is a lock — once you've said a row is a person, the nightly pass never
judges it again.

**Anyone you've already acted on is never judged in the first place.** If
you've starred them, put them on your watchlist, set a keep-in-touch rhythm,
recorded reaching out to them, or written a single note about them, Dhaga
treats that as settled and leaves the contact out of the sweep entirely. The
only rows it looks at are the ones nobody has ever touched.

When Dhaga can't tell from the record — a bare first name and nothing else —
it says so and leaves the contact alone rather than guessing. Only a confident
"not a person" ever suppresses anything.
